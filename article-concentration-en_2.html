Is Your Wealth Riding on a Single Bet?

Fresco Su · October 2026 · 8 min read · Also in 繁體中文

I love researching and talking about investing. Reading a company’s financial statements gives me the same satisfaction as reading a good novel. That passion also got me thinking about diversification and concentration risk at a young age. I still remember opening an account with HSBC in Hong Kong when I was 26, because there were far more options for investing in China equity funds there. It was my first attempt at “international diversification.”

Later, I worked for British American Tobacco, a major constituent of the FTSE index. Whenever I introduced the company to new colleagues, I opened with its footprint across more than 180 markets worldwide. Diversification has never been only about not putting all your eggs in one basket. It is also a reminder that the world is much bigger than what is right in front of us.

So when I read the following news, it struck a chord. In May 2026, explaining the employee bonus to staff, TSMC Chairman C.C. Wei said: “Use your bonus to buy TSMC shares, and your future livelihood will be secure.” From the company’s point of view, this is a statement full of confidence, and a perfectly reasonable one. But put the same sentence on a family’s balance sheet, and it means something quite different.

In “Getting rich and staying rich,” I wrote that what makes people rich is often concentration, and what keeps them rich is diversification. Concentration in itself is not wrong; a great deal of wealth has been built exactly this way. A founder who pours everything into one company, an engineer who has spent more than a decade at a good firm: these are choices worth respecting.

The real problem is that concentration is often not something we choose. It forms bit by bit, naturally, without our noticing. It is hard to see for two reasons. First, it is usually rewarded: the more money a position makes, the less likely we are to question it. Second, it often happens by default: salaries are paid in automatically, shares vest automatically, dividends are reinvested automatically, and busy as we are, we rarely find the time to stop and think.

Below are ten situations I hear about often, turned into ten questions. Ask yourself each one and answer “yes,” “no” or “not sure.” You don’t need precise numbers. Just be honest with yourself.

1. Do your salary, bonus and shareholdings all come from the same company?

Does your main cash flow come from a single source, a single employer?

Picture an engineer who has worked at a tech company for more than ten years. The monthly salary comes from the company. The year-end bonus and profit-sharing come from the company. The largest holding in the portfolio is company stock. A few years ago, the year-end bonus paid the down payment on a home, which was also bought near the office. The pay and bonuses are enviable. But what happens when the good times end, and a falling share price, a shrinking bonus and the risk of layoffs all arrive at once?

Risks tend to travel in packs. Like the butterfly effect, one event sets off the next. This is exactly what makes concentration so dangerous.

2. Is 0050 or another index ETF your largest single holding?

Could what you think of as diversification just be concentration in different packaging? In recent years, many investors have switched to index ETFs such as 0050, SPY and VOO, believing this makes them diversified. But data from early 2026 shows that TSMC makes up about 60% of 0050, and seven other Taiwan-listed ETFs have a TSMC weighting above 50%. US indices show a similar pattern: the top ten constituents alone account for more than 30%.

The same thing happens to people who hold several ETFs at once. The names are different, the tickers are different, but open up the holdings and the top ten may overlap by more than half. Diversification is not about how many funds you own. It is about whether those positions will fall on the same day. You think you have spread your risk, but you haven’t spread it at all.

3. Are your assets concentrated in today’s star stocks or hottest themes?

VIA Technologies in 2000, HTC in 2011, Largan Precision in 2017: each was once the highest-priced stock on the Taiwan market, and each once made people believe “this time is different.” They were all good companies, but share prices tend to peak precisely when everyone is most confident.

The bestselling book Built to Last picked 18 visionary companies in 1994. About ten years after it was published, nearly half had slipped in performance and reputation. Those companies were once great, but today most of them are no longer near the top of the market-value rankings.

Financial TV shows everywhere now tell you which company is best and which theme is hottest. My father often calls me to say I should hurry and buy a certain stock, after watching a programme or chatting with friends. This isn’t necessarily ill-intentioned. It is a matter of incentives: people who live on audience traffic need you to always have something new to buy. But keeping your wealth doesn’t require someone handing you hot tips. It requires a structure that doesn’t depend on guessing the winner.

4. Is more than half of your net worth the home you live in?

Owning your home brings a sense of security, but it has three features that are often overlooked: it can’t be sold in parts, it is illiquid, and it is usually a single asset held with mortgage leverage.

Taiwan sits on an earthquake belt. From the 921 earthquake to the April 3, 2024 earthquake in Hualien, we all know a building’s value can change in a matter of seconds. Taiwan also sits on the geopolitical first island chain. This question isn’t about predicting disaster. It is insurance thinking: if the worst does happen, do you have a plan?

5. Are most of your assets in one country and priced in one currency?

Your home is in Taiwan, your savings are in Taiwan, your stocks are in Taiwan, all priced in New Taiwan dollars. It’s natural, and it’s convenient.

But it’s worth asking: are your assets overly concentrated in a single country and a single currency? Your children may study abroad. You may want to retire in Thailand or Spain. If your spending will be in foreign currencies while your assets are all in Taiwan dollars, exchange rates will make part of your decisions for you, without your choosing. The bigger question is this: if something happens that affects only one country, whether a natural disaster, financial turmoil or some other upheaval, do you have any assets that are not in the same place and not in the same currency?

6. Are most of your foreign-currency assets held in US dollar insurance policies?

Many Taiwanese families keep their money in US dollar savings insurance, and for good reasons: it’s stable, it offers protection, and it’s a form of asset allocation. But from a concentration standpoint, such a policy carries three exposures at once: the US dollar exchange rate, the credit of a single insurance company, and limited liquidity if you surrender the policy.

Even the insurance giants couldn’t escape foreign-exchange losses. When the Taiwan dollar surged in April and May 2025, the life insurance industry’s roughly NT$23 trillion in overseas assets suffered heavy FX losses. Insurers have reserve mechanisms to absorb the hit on their books, but when your policy’s value is converted back into Taiwan dollars, the exchange rate won’t absorb anything for you. This question isn’t telling you to cancel your policy. It’s asking you to recognise that if nearly all your foreign-currency assets sit in policies, your diversification is actually concentrated in one company, one currency, and one instrument that is hard to access early.

7. Do most of your annual dividends come from the same industry?

Holding stocks for dividends is a very common way to invest in Taiwan, and it makes dividends feel like a steady passive income. But dividends come from company profits, and profits come from the business cycle. In 2022, massive claims on COVID insurance policies led many of the financial stocks that dividend investors know well to cut their payouts sharply the following year. That was the year many people discovered that the dividends they thought were spread across several stocks actually came mostly from a single industry.

Dividends aren’t extra money, either. Every time a stock goes ex-dividend, its price drops by the same amount. What really matters is total return, and whether these income streams shrink at the same time in a bad year. A dividend is not a guarantee. It carries the same risk as the share price, just in a different form.

8. Have you personally guaranteed a loan for your own company?

This question is for business owners. For many owners of small and medium-sized companies, the business is their largest asset, and through personal guarantees it also becomes their largest potential liability. Banks commonly require the owner to provide a personal guarantee when approving a loan, and few people think twice when signing.

When the business is doing well, the two have nothing to do with each other. When the business runs into trouble, the bank comes after your family’s assets. This is assets and liabilities concentrated in the same place, and it usually doesn’t appear on any financial statement. If you can’t remember which guarantees you’ve signed, for how much, or when they expire, that in itself is an answer.

9. Do you directly hold US stocks or US-listed ETFs worth more than US$60,000 in total?

Buying US stocks has become popular in recent years, but few people realise that this also places part of their estate under the US tax system. Under current US rules, US assets held by non-US tax residents are subject to US estate tax on the portion above US$60,000, at rates of up to 40%.

This is a kind of concentration that is rarely discussed: not a concentration of assets, but a concentration of legal jurisdiction. Where are your assets registered, and which country’s laws govern them?

10. Is there only one person in your family who knows the accounts, the passwords and the investment decisions?

This is the most overlooked concentration of all: not assets, but people. In many families, every account, password, policy and investment decision lives in one person’s head, usually the one who earns the most and is the busiest.

If that person falls ill, loses capacity or passes away suddenly, the family may not even know which accounts exist, let alone how to handle them. The assets don’t disappear, but they can be frozen and out of reach, and the family has to deal with the most complicated matters at the hardest possible moment. Have you made sure at least one other person in your family knows where your assets are?

Now count how many questions you answered “yes” or “not sure.” “Not sure” counts too: very often, the biggest risk isn’t the position you know about, but the one you’ve never looked at carefully.

0–2: Your wealth is relatively diversified.

3–5: You may already be standing in some form of concentrated risk.

6 or more: Your wealth may be riding on a single bet more than you realise.

Concentration helped build the wealth you have today, and there is nothing to regret about that. The problem is not having a plan. You can keep believing in your company, your home and the markets you know, but your whole family’s future doesn’t need to be exposed to a single risk.
